# Worldbuilding Terms Codex: Sharp Distinctions, Grounded Examples & The WYGWYL Engine

This document serves as the master reference manual for worldbuilders, writers, narrative designers, spatial computing engineers, and future AI agents. It clarifies the terminology used to describe the creation, organization, analysis, and computation of fictional worlds, sorting through historically adjacent and often conflated terms.

Part I provides the universal theoretical definitions, etymologies, and the Master Reference Table.  
Part II details every term with classical Humanities, History, and Media examples, concluded with deep architectural and code-level groundings in **WYGWYL · Atlantis Urbanism**.

## Sharp Distinctions (The Shortlist)

*   **Legendarium**: The body of myth, language, and history belonging to a world (mythic scale).
*   **World Bible**: The master reference document for a fictional world (the world as the primary object).
*   **Story Bible**: A guide to the rules, characters, and continuity of a specific narrative (production-oriented).
*   **Series Bible**: The blueprint for an ongoing, episodic show or book series (repeatable engine).
*   **Franchise Bible**: A cross-media IP control document (coordinates games, books, toys, films).
*   **Canon**: The officially accepted truth of the world (the boundary of what "counts").
*   **Continuity**: The internal consistency of facts, dates, and events over time.
*   **Lore**: Accumulated background knowledge, customs, and rumors (often informal/diegetic).
*   **Sourcebook**: A reference book providing usable setting facts, designed for play or adaptation.
*   **Setting Guide**: A practical geographical or contextual guide to a place or era.
*   **Campaign Setting**: An interactive setting designed specifically for gameplay and hooks.
*   **Atlas**: A map-centered guide emphasizing spatial layouts, routes, and geography.
*   **Gazetteer**: A text-heavy alphabetical dictionary of place names, landmarks, and populations.
*   **Codex**: A formal, bound book of collected, official, or sacred knowledge.
*   **Compendium**: A broad collection of world facts compressed into a single weight.
*   **Concordance**: An index of terms and references mapping where they appear in a text.
*   **Encyclopedia**: An alphabetical, systematic reference of all knowledge in a world.
*   **Lexicon**: A vocabulary list, dictionary, or grammatical study of specialized languages.
*   **Chronology**: A reasoned, standardized ordering of events through time.
*   **Chronicle**: A chronological, story-like narrative history of events.
*   **Mythos**: The underlying sacred stories, god-structures, and meanings.
*   **Cosmology**: The physical and metaphysical structure of reality.
*   **Cosmogony**: The birth or origin narrative of the universe.
*   **Diegesis**: The fictional world as experienced by the characters from within.
*   **Storyworld**: The mental model of a world implied by a narrative.
*   **Universe**: A broad fictional reality containing multiple separate story systems.
*   **Setting**: The specific time, place, and social environment of an action.
*   **Production Bible**: The physical, visual, and operational rules for creating media.
*   **Narrative Design Document**: A plan mapping how story systems translate into game mechanics.

### 1. Legendarium
*   **Sharp Distinction**: A body of legends, myths, histories, languages, places, and stories belonging to one imagined world. Best for Tolkien-scale mythic worlds.
*   **Etymology**: From *legend*, via Latin *legenda* ("things to be read"), from *legere* ("to read, gather, select"). Root-signal: *Collected sacred/heroic readings*.
*   **Humanities Example**: J.R.R. Tolkien's Middle-earth corpus (including *The Silmarillion*, *The Hobbit*, and *The Lord of the Rings* along with constructed languages like Quenya and Sindarin), studied academically as a cohesive mythological system.
*   **History Example**: The Arthurian Matter of Britain, a medieval legendarium combining Celtic mythology, French romance, and pseudo-histories of King Arthur and the Round Table.
*   **Media Example**: The expanded Middle-earth media universe (films, video games like *Shadow of Mordor*, and series like *The Rings of Power*), which all systematically draw from Tolkien's original textual legendarium.
*   **WYGWYL Atlantis Grounding**: The deep mythos of submerged Atlantis—the ancient Afrofuturist maritime empire that adapted to toxic lead-skies and oceanic flooding. ### 2. World Bible
*   **Sharp Distinction**: A master reference document for a fictional world. Best when the world itself is the main object.
*   **Etymology**: *World* from Old English *woruld* ("age of man"); *Bible* from Greek *biblia* ("the books"). Root-signal: *The authoritative book of a human age/world*.
*   **Humanities Example**: Ursula K. Le Guin's comprehensive preparatory notes for the Earthsea archipelago, outlining the naming rules, magic principles, islands, and cultures.
*   **History Example**: The **Domesday Book** (1086), a comprehensive survey of England's landholders, livestock, values, and rights commissioned by William the Conqueror to serve as the definitive "world record" of his new domain.
*   **Media Example**: The *Avatar: The Last Airbender* series bible, which details the lore of the four nations, rules of elemental bending, history of Avatars, and the spiritual framework of the world.
*   **WYGWYL Atlantis Grounding**: The structural and material rules encoded in [`shots/build_polygonal_district.py`](file:///Users/gaia/Downloads/WYGWYL-WORLD-ARCH/shots/build_polygonal_district.py). ### 3. Story Bible
*   **Sharp Distinction**: A guide to the rules, characters, tone, and continuity of a story or series. More production-oriented than a legendarium.
*   **Etymology**: *Story* from Greek/Latin *historia* ("inquiry, knowledge"); *Bible* from Greek *biblia* ("the books"). Root-signal: *The authoritative account-book of a story system*.
*   **Humanities Example**: The Homeric epic tradition surrounding the Trojan War, which acted as a narrative guide for oral poets, specifying character traits (e.g., "swift-footed Achilles"), divine interventions, and plot milestones.
*   **History Example**: Medieval hagiographical collections (saints' lives), which provided Christian writers with standardized story templates, virtues, and structural arcs for recording miraculous deeds.
*   **Media Example**: The writer's guide/story bible for the television show *Lost*, defining the island's mysterious properties, character flashbacks, and the rules of pacing for revelations.

### 4. Series Bible
*   **Sharp Distinction**: A document for an ongoing TV, book, comic, or game series. Focuses on repeatability: characters, arcs, episode logic, tone.
*   **Etymology**: *Series* from Latin *series* ("row, chain, sequence"), from *serere* ("to join/link"). Root-signal: *A chain of repeatable installments*.
*   **Humanities Example**: Honoré de Balzac's structural plan for *La Comédie humaine*, a linked series of over 90 novels that reused characters across different social classes and settings to create a cohesive literary world.
*   **History Example**: Regnal annals or dynasty lists maintained by court scribes, which established a structural pattern where each king's reign was recorded using consistent metrics of battles, building projects, and accessions.
*   **Media Example**: The series bible for *The Wire*, which outlines the institutional focus of each season (police, docks, school, media) and details how the cyclical nature of Baltimore's drug trade functions.

### 5. Franchise Bible
*   **Sharp Distinction**: A broader guide for an IP across many media: films, games, books, merch, spin-offs. Bigger than a story bible.
*   **Etymology**: *Franchise* from Old French *franchise* ("freedom, exemption, right, privilege"). Root-signal: *Authorized rights and rules across media uses*.
*   **Humanities Example**: Sherlock Holmes scholarship and "The Game" (treating Holmes as a real person), which establishes boundaries between Sir Arthur Conan Doyle's original 56 stories/4 novels and the vast sea of adaptations and pastiches.

*   **Humanities Example**: The chronological consistency in Marcel Proust's *In Search of Lost Time*, where recurring memories and social networks must align across seven volumes.
*   **History Example**: The meticulous records of Chinese dynastic successions, where court historians resolved timeline discrepancies to maintain an unbroken thread of the Mandate of Heaven.

### 8. Lore
*   **Sharp Distinction**: The accumulated background knowledge of a world: histories, rumors, myths, customs, factions, hidden facts. Less formal than canon.
*   **Etymology**: From Old English *lār* ("teaching, instruction, learning"), linked to a root sense of "track/furrow." Root-signal: *Knowledge handed down as a path*.
*   **Humanities Example**: The study of urban legends and folklore (e.g., the Brothers Grimm collecting traditional German oral tales to document cultural lore).
*   **History Example**: The oral traditions of the West African Griots, who memorized generations of genealogies, battles, and treaties as the living history/lore of their empires.

### 9. Sourcebook
*   **Sharp Distinction**: A reference book that gives usable information about a setting, often for games. Best when the world is meant to be played or adapted.
*   **Etymology**: *Source* from Old French *sourse* ("rising, beginning"); *book* from Old English *bōc*. Root-signal: *The fountainhead reference book*.
*   **Humanities Example**: A primary sourcebook used in history classes, compiling translated letters, laws, and diaries from a specific historical period (e.g., *The Middle Ages: A Sourcebook*).
*   **History Example**: Meticulous travelogues like Ibn Battuta’s *Rihla*, which served as a practical sourcebook for geography, customs, and political climates for 14th-century Islamic travelers.

### 18. Lexicon
*   **Sharp Distinction**: A vocabulary list, often for specialized terms or languages. Best when words themselves matter.
*   **Etymology**: From Greek *lexikon biblion* ("word-book"), from *lexis* ("word, phrase, diction"). Root-signal: *The word-system of a world*.
*   **Humanities Example**: The *Qenya Lexicon* compiled by J.R.R. Tolkien, where he drafted the roots, etymologies, and grammar of his first constructed Elvish language.
*   **History Example**: The *Lexicon of Suidas* (an alternate name for the Suda) or medieval Latin lexicons used to translate legal and ecclesiastical terminology across Europe.
*   **Media Example**: The "Terminology of the Imperium" glossary at the back of Frank Herbert's *Dune*, defining specialized words like *Kwisatz Haderach*, *Bene Gesserit*, and *Melange*.

### 19. Chronology
*   **Sharp Distinction**: Ordered events through time. More formal than a timeline.
*   **Etymology**: From Greek *chronos* ("time") and *-logy* ("discourse, study"). Root-signal: *The reasoned study and ordering of time*.
*   **Humanities Example**: Joseph Scaliger's *De emendatione temporum* (1583), which founded modern historical chronology by synchronizing ancient calendars (Egyptian, Greek, Roman) with the Julian calendar.
*   **History Example**: The Eusebian Canon tables (4th century), which synchronized the timelines of Hebrew, Egyptian, Persian, and Roman history side-by-side.
*   **Media Example**: The Star Wars timeline system using "BBY" (Before the Battle of Yavin) and "ABY" (After the Battle of Yavin) to organize millennia of galactic history.

### 20. Chronicle
*   **Sharp Distinction**: A narrative history of events. More storylike than annals.
*   **Etymology**: From Greek *khronika biblia* ("books of time"). Root-signal: *History told in chronological, narrative order*.
*   **Humanities Example**: Jean Froissart's *Chronicles* (14th century), which provides a highly narrative, eyewitness-style account of the chivalry and horrors of the Hundred Years' War.
*   **History Example**: The *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, a collection of annals in Old English chronicling the history of the Anglo-Saxons from the birth of Christ to the 12th century.
*   **Media Example**: *The Chronicles of Narnia* by C.S. Lewis, which narrates the history of the world of Narnia from its singing creation to its ultimate destruction.

### 21. Mythos
*   **Sharp Distinction**: The underlying mythic system of a world: gods, origins, monsters, sacred stories, cosmic meanings.
*   **Etymology**: From Greek *mythos* ("speech, tale, story, saga"). Root-signal: *Spoken sacred story-system*.
*   **Humanities Example**: Claude Lévi-Strauss’s structural study of indigenous mythology in *Mythologiques*, which argues that myths reflect universal structures of the human mind.
*   **History Example**: The Homeric and Hesiodic Greek mythos, establishing the relationships, domains, and sacred narratives of the Olympian gods.
*   **Media Example**: The *Cthulhu Mythos*, the cosmic horror setting initiated by H.P. Lovecraft and expanded by other writers, unified by themes of cosmic indifference and forbidden knowledge.

### 22. Cosmology
*   **Sharp Distinction**: The structure of reality: heavens, worlds, planes, afterlife, gods, metaphysics.
*   **Etymology**: From Greek *kosmos* ("order, arrangement, world") and *-logy* ("discourse, study"). Root-signal: *The logic of cosmic order*.
*   **Humanities Example**: Dante Alighieri’s *Divine Comedy*, which illustrates the medieval Christian cosmological structure of Hell (nine circles), Purgatory (nine terraces), and Heaven (nine spheres).
*   **History Example**: The geocentric Ptolemaic model of the universe, which dominated European astronomical cosmology from antiquity through the Middle Ages.
*   **Media Example**: The "Great Wheel" cosmology of *Dungeons & Dragons*, detailing how the Material Plane connects to the Astral Plane, Ethereal Plane, and various Inner and Outer planes.

### 23. Cosmogony
*   **Sharp Distinction**: The origin story of the universe. Cosmology is the structure; cosmogony is how it began.
*   **Etymology**: From Greek *kosmos* ("world") and *-gonia* ("birth, generation"). Root-signal: *The birth story of the cosmos*.
*   **Humanities Example**: Hesiod's *Theogony* (8th century BC), a poem describing the birth of the Greek gods and the origin of the cosmos from Chaos.
*   **History Example**: The Mesopotamian *Enûma Eliš* (Babylonian creation myth), recording the cosmogonical battle where Marduk splits the carcass of the dragon Tiamat to form the earth and sky.
*   **Media Example**: The *Ainulindalë* in Tolkien's *The Silmarillion*, which presents a cosmogony where Eru Ilúvatar and the Ainur sing the universe (*Eä*) into existence.
*   **WYGWYL Atlantis Grounding**: The historical narrative of Atlantis's rebirth following the oceanic inundation, where survivors engineered modular container architectures and elevated viaducts above the permanent sodium haze.

### 24. Diegesis
*   **Sharp Distinction**: The story-world as experienced by characters. Best for theory, film, media, and narrative analysis.
*   **Etymology**: From Greek *diegesis* ("narration, storytelling"). Root-signal: *The world from inside the telling*.
*   **Humanities Example**: Plato's *Republic*, where he contrasts *diegesis* (pure narration in the poet's own voice) with *mimesis* (imitative representation, where characters speak for themselves).
*   **History Example**: Analyzing historical testimonies (like the letters of Pliny the Younger about the eruption of Vesuvius) to map the narrative diegesis of the Roman citizens experiencing the disaster.
*   **Media Example**: The distinction between diegetic elements in film (like a song playing from a radio in a car that the characters can hear) and non-diegetic elements (like the dramatic orchestral score).

### 25. Storyworld
*   **Sharp Distinction**: The imagined world implied by a story, including places, characters, rules, history, and possible events.
*   **Etymology**: Compound of *story* (from *historia*) and *world* (from *woruld*). Root-signal: *The full human world implied by a narrative*.
*   **Humanities Example**: David Herman’s cognitive narrative theory in *Story Logic*, which defines a storyworld as the mental model readers build to track characters, events, and spatial layouts in text.
*   **History Example**: Reconstructing the mental storyworld of medieval European villagers, where geographic space was filled with localized demons, saintly relics, and active miracles.
*   **Media Example**: The Cyberpunk storyworld, which spans Mike Pondsmith's original tabletop RPGs, CD Projekt Red's video game *Cyberpunk 2077*, and the anime *Cyberpunk: Edgerunners*, all sharing the same corporate-ruled dystopian reality.

### 26. Universe
*   **Sharp Distinction**: The broad fictional reality containing many stories. Bigger and more flexible than "setting."
*   **Etymology**: From Latin *universus* ("all together, turned into one"). Root-signal: *Totality unified into a single whole*.
*   **Humanities Example**: Mikhail Bakhtin's literary theory of the "polyphonic universe," particularly in his studies of Fyodor Dostoevsky’s novels, where each character represents a distinct voice and ideological world.
*   **History Example**: The transition from the closed medieval cosmos to the infinite Newtonian universe, shifting society's scientific world view from a theological center to an endless space governed by physical law.
*   **Media Example**: The Marvel Cinematic Universe (MCU), a sprawling media universe consisting of dozens of interconnected films, TV shows, and characters that all exist within the same shared timeline.

### 27. Setting
*   **Sharp Distinction**: The time, place, and social world where a story happens. Narrower than universe.

*   **History Example**: The 19th-century industrial mill towns of Lancashire, England, which served as the physical and socio-economic setting that birthed Karl Marx's critique of capital.
*   **Media Example**: The setting of the Overlook Hotel in Stephen King's *The Shining*, a haunted, isolated location that acts as a psychological catalyst driving the protagonist insane.
